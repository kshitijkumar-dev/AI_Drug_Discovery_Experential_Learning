01 · INTRODUCTION

Why Drug Discovery Needs AI

Finding a medicine is slow, costly and risky. AI does not decide which drug is good — it helps experts look at the most promising options first.

🧠

Pattern Learning

Machine learning and deep learning learn patterns from molecular structures, lab assays and patient records, then use them to make predictions.

🔍

Early Filtering

Virtual screening ranks huge libraries of molecules on a computer, so weak candidates are removed before expensive wet-lab tests.

🤝

Human–AI Synergy

Algorithms rank promising options; biologists and clinicians validate safety and effect in the lab and in patients.

03 · SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

Four-Layer AI Discovery Architecture

A conceptual architecture drawn from the reviewed sources [1], [2]. Results from the last layer flow back to the first, so models can be retrained.

1 · Data Layer

Raw inputs: chemical libraries, protein sequences and structures, assay results, patient records.

2 · Model Layer

Algorithms: machine learning, deep neural networks, generative models (e.g. GENTRL), reinforcement learning.

3 · Task Layer

Applied jobs: target finding, virtual screening, de novo design, synthesis planning, drug repurposing.

4 · Validation Layer

Real-world proof: wet-lab experiments, animal studies and human clinical trials.

↻ Closed-loop feedback: new results retrain the models
04 · CORE APPLICATIONS

Key AI Techniques in Modern Pharmacology

The main application areas found in the literature.

🧬

Protein Structure Prediction

AlphaFold predicts a protein's 3D shape from its amino acid sequence, revealing binding pockets.

🔬

Virtual Screening

Models rank very large libraries (>107 million molecules in one study) so labs test only the top candidates.

⚛️

De Novo Molecule Design

Generative models create brand-new molecules with wanted properties, such as potency and low toxicity.

💊

Drug Repurposing

Finds new uses for existing drugs — e.g. halicin, found as an antibiotic in the Drug Repurposing Hub.

09 · CRITICAL EVALUATION

Limitations & Future Priorities

Key Challenges


Data & human bias: narrow training sets reduce generalisation to novel targets [4].


Phase II bottleneck: models cannot yet fully simulate complex human physiology.


Review scope: literature synthesis of 8 sources; Phase II sample is limited; no new experiments or user studies.

Future Roadmap


Explainable AI: confidence scores (like AlphaFold's pLDDT) build scientist trust.


Broader datasets: diverse, less biased data and larger Phase II/III studies.


Interdisciplinary teams: engineers, biologists and chemists working together.

AI accelerates early discovery, while wet labs confirm human safety

Published studies show AI cutting molecular design to 21 days, predicting protein folds within 0.96 Å and reaching 80–90% Phase I success — yet Phase II efficacy (~40%) and data bias show that computational models must partner with laboratory and clinical validation.
